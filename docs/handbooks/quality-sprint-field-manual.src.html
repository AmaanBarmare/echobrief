How to use this manual

This is not a reading document. It is a worksheet. Every mission ends in a file you commit to the repo — a scorecard, a report, or a pull request. If a day ends with no artefact, that day did not happen.

What this week is

EchoBrief works end-to-end. A bot joins a call, the audio is transcribed, and a summary lands by email. That is proven. What is not proven is whether it is any good — across languages, across long meetings, and against the note-takers we are competing with.

Your job is to convert opinions into measurements.

The one rule that matters

Don't change code you're less than 95% sure about. This is a live product with real users. A wrong fix is worse than an unfixed bug you documented clearly.

When under 95%: write down what you observed, what you think is happening, and what you'd change. Then ask. That's a win, not a failure.

Bias warning — read twice

You will be tempted to make EchoBrief look good. Resist it hard. The most valuable thing you can hand over on Friday is a list of things that are broken and a number attached to each one. Finding nothing is the only failing outcome. Every real product has failures at this stage; if your report has none, you tested too gently.

The machine

How a meeting becomes a summary

Nine stages. Every mission this week is really a question about one or two of them. Learn where each mission bites and you'll debug ten times faster.

1 · Bot joins
Recall.ai bot enters the call and records
›
2 · Audio ready
audio_mixed.done webhook fires
›
3 · Download
16 kHz mono mp3 + Recall's own transcript for speaker names
›
4 · Split
Vercel ffmpeg cuts 300 s chunks (stream-copy)
5 · Sarvam
One multi-file job, translate mode, diarization on
›
6 · Stitch
Chunks rejoined, timestamps offset by index × 300 s
›
7 · Speakers
Sarvam segments matched to Recall's speaker timeline
›
8 · GPT-4o-mini
Summary, actions, decisions, risks, timeline
›
9 · Deliver
Saved to DB, emailed as a digest
Where each mission bites
M1Stage 5. Does Sarvam handle each language? Does translate mode make the language promise true or false?
M2Stages 4–6. Chunking and stitching. Long audio is where this pipeline has historically broken.
M3Stages 5, 7, 8. Word accuracy, speaker attribution, and whether GPT's claims are actually in the transcript.
M4Stage 8–9. What we compute and show after the meeting.

Two things that will confuse you on day one

Why chunking exists. Sarvam's saaras:v3 silently returns an empty transcript for long audio — no error, just nothing. A 47-minute file fails; the same file cut into 5-minute pieces succeeds. So we cut everything over ~6 minutes into 300-second chunks. This is a vendor bug we work around, not a design choice.

Why there are two transcripts. Recall produces its own rough transcript. We don't show it — we use it purely to learn who spoke when, because Sarvam gives good words but anonymous speakers. Stage 7 is the marriage of the two, and it is the least reliable stage in the system.

Failure is designed-in, and that matters for your testing

If Sarvam returns nothing, an error, or the known KeyError: 'timestamps' bug, the system silently falls back to Whisper and carries on. So "we got a transcript" does not mean "Sarvam worked". For every test you run, record which engine produced the result — otherwise Mission 1's language findings are meaningless.

Harness vs Eval — you will need this distinction all week

The harness asks "did the machine run?". The evals ask "was what came out correct?". A meeting can complete with status completed, a green harness, and a summary that is total nonsense. Missions 1–3 live almost entirely on the eval side of that line.

H4Auto-detect (language_code: "unknown") is weakest on code-mixed audio, where Hinglish may be detected as English and the Hindi half quietly dropped.
H5The landing-page list shows 21 Indian languages plus English, omitting Nepali — so "22 Indian languages" is arguably miscounted. Confirm by reading src/components/landing/Languages.tsx.
Method
1
Build the fixture set — 24 clips
One 60–120 s clip per language on the landing page, plus two code-mixed clips (Hinglish, Tanglish) since that's what real Indian meetings sound like. Each needs a human-verified reference transcript.
2
Run every fixture three ways
(a) production settings — translate + unknown; (b) translate with the language pinned; (c) transcribe with the language pinned, i.e. output in the native script. Run (c) even though it isn't what prod does — it separates "Sarvam can't hear this language" from "our config is wrong", and those have completely different fixes.
3
Record the detected language and confidence
Sarvam returns a language_probability when detecting. Log it. A confident wrong detection is a much more serious finding than a low-confidence one.
4

Use the 0–4 rubric overleaf. Where you have a reference translation, also compute WER. Get a native speaker to sanity-check any language you don't speak — one 10-minute call per language is cheap and it is the only thing that makes the score credible.
5
Push the best and the worst language through the whole pipeline
Transcript quality isn't the product — the summary is. Take your highest and lowest scoring languages, run them through to insights, and check whether a mediocre transcript still yields a usable summary. Sometimes it does. That finding changes what we fix first.

Mission 2 · Long meetingsTue PM – Wed AM
02
Does it survive a 45-minute meeting?
Stages 4–6 · split-audio → Sarvam → stitch
Tue PM – Wed AM
The question

Real meetings are 30–60 minutes. At what duration does quality start to degrade, and at what duration does it break outright?

Background you need

Sarvam's saaras:v3 silently returns an empty transcript on long audio — a confirmed vendor bug. A 47-minute file returned nothing; the same file in 5–6 minute chunks worked. So anything over ~6 minutes is cut into 300-second chunks by a Vercel ffmpeg function, submitted as one multi-file job, then stitched back together with each chunk's timestamps offset by index × 300.

That machinery is where long meetings break. A 45-minute meeting is 9 chunks; one bad chunk is 11% of the meeting silently missing, and nothing in the UI will tell the user.

Four failure modes to hunt for

Empty chunkOne chunk returns nothing; the stitch succeeds anyway and 5 minutes vanish
Offset driftTimestamps after chunk 1 are wrong, so the timeline and speaker mapping slide out of sync
Boundary lossA sentence spanning a cut is mangled or duplicated at both edges
Summary flatteningTranscript is fine, but GPT compresses 45 minutes into the same 5 bullets it gives a 10-minute call — early topics disappear
Method — the duration ladder
1
Build one long fixture, then cut it down
Take a single ~90-minute clear multi-speaker recording (a panel discussion, a podcast, a recorded internal call) and produce truncations at 5, 15, 30, 45, 60, 90 minutes. Same content, same voices — so duration is the only variable. Comparing six different recordings would tell you nothing.
2
Push each through the real production path
Splitter → Sarvam → webhook → stitch → insights. Not the local mock. If the splitter is bypassed you're not testing the thing that breaks.
3
Measure five things per run
Chunk count · non-empty chunks / total chunks · total transcript characters · whether timestamps increase monotonically across every boundary · wall-clock from submission to completed.
4
Check for silent content loss
Characters should scale roughly linearly with duration. If 30 min gives ~28,000 characters and 45 min gives ~30,000, you have found missing content — even though the meeting shows as completed. This is the single most valuable bug you can find this week.
5
Read the summaries side by side
For 15, 45 and 90 minutes: does the summary of the 90-minute run actually mention what happened in the first 15 minutes? Count action items found at each duration. Watch for the count going flat as the meeting gets longer — that's the flattening failure.
Also check the edges, not just the middle

Two known sharp corners. Under 6 minutes, chunking is skipped entirely — a different code path, so test 5 minutes deliberately. Whisper OOMs in the edge function above roughly 15 MB of audio, so if a long meeting falls back from Sarvam to Whisper it may die a second time. Force that path once and record what the user would see.

Mission 3 · AccuracyWed PM – Thu
03
Is the summary actually correct?
Stages 5, 7 & 8 · words, speakers, insights
Wed PM – Thu
The question

If a user reads only our summary and never the transcript, will they be correctly informed — or confidently misled?

Measure four things separately — they fail for different reasons and have different fixes
1 · Word accuracyDid it hear the words? → fix = Sarvam config or audio quality
2 · Speaker attributionDid it get who said what? → fix = the Recall-timeline mapping in sarvam-webhook
3 · ExtractionDid it find the real action items and decisions? → fix = the GPT prompt
4 · FaithfulnessDid it invent anything? → fix = prompt constraints + hallucination checks

A single "accuracy: 78%" number is useless to whoever has to fix it. Four numbers are actionable.

Method
1
Build a golden set of 5 meetings
Aim for: one clean English 2-person call · one Hinglish 3-person call · one noisy call (background sound, a phone speaker) · one long call from Mission 2 · one meeting where people talk over each other. That last one is where every note-taker on the market falls apart, so it's where you find something worth reporting.
2
Write ground truth by hand
For each: the true transcript, the true speaker labels, and a human list of every action item and decision. This is slow — budget three hours — and it is the entire foundation of the mission. Don't let an LLM write it; you'd be grading the model against itself.
3
Compute WER per meeting
Normalise both sides first — lowercase, strip punctuation — then use jiwer. Report overall WER and a separate error count for names, numbers and dates, because those are the words that actually matter in a meeting summary.
4
Score speaker attribution
Percentage of segments assigned to the right person. Note separately whether any SPEAKER_00-style label leaked through to the UI — that's a visible product bug, not just an accuracy number.
5
Score action items with precision and recall
Recall = of the real action items, how many did we find? Precision = of the ones we listed, how many were real? Low recall means users miss things. Low precision means users stop trusting the tool. State clearly which one we have.
6
Hunt hallucinations deliberately
For every claim in a summary, find the supporting line in the transcript. Pay special attention to owners ("Rahul will send the deck" — did Rahul actually say that, or did the model assign it?) and to sentiment_score, which is a pure model guess with no measurement behind it.
7
Fold your golden set into the eval harness
python3 scripts/evals/run_evals.py --snapshot <meeting-id> pulls a real meeting into the eval dataset. Do this for every golden meeting. This is the highest-leverage hour of your week — it turns your one-off testing into a permanent regression gate.

Weak
"Kannada didn't work very well, the transcript looked bad."
Strong
"Kannada scored 1/4 (WER 41%, fixture kn-IN-news-90s.mp3, Sarvam job abc-123). Auto-detect returned te-IN at 0.81 confidence — confidently wrong, not uncertain. Things that will happen to you

Escalate immediately — don't wait for standup

  • Anything touching real customer data

How to ask a good question

Four lines, always:

  1. Goal — what I was trying to do
  2. Attempt — the exact command or change
  3. Expected vs actual — with the error pasted verbatim, not paraphrased
  4. My theory — what I think is happening

Point four is the one that turns a question into a conversation between engineers.

Cost discipline

Sarvam, OpenAI and Recall all bill per use. Recall bot minutes are the expensive one — prefer fixture files over live bots wherever the test allows it. Keep a running tally of what you've spent in raw/spend.md and flag it the moment you're near the agreed ceiling.
